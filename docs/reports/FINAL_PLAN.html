§1What changed today

Two board runs and a read of the RTL moved the whole plan.

Your three board runs give a clean per-invocation cost, and it is remarkably flat: 262 µs at 128 px, 255 µs at 192, 251 µs at 320. Five system calls per invocation puts a single call at about 50 µs, and multiplying that by the call count reproduces the measured GEMM time at all three canvases to within 4%. The GEMM loop is not computing. It is making system calls.

These supersede the 528.8 s in the Stage 3B report by a factor of about three. Whatever changed between machines, every timing claim we make on 1 September must come from this table.

The RTL never required the k0 loop

gemm_run issues (M/8)×(N/8)×(K/8) invocations. The K factor is the expensive one: measured on the deployed graph it averages 38 K-tiles per output tile. But reading the accelerator source, nothing fixes K at 8:

  1. In axis_sa.sv, the accumulator is reset by m_first, which follows vlast — so an accumulation runs until a last beat, however many beats that is.
  2. In top_axi_int.v, s_last = s_k_tlast & s_x_tlast — the DMA asserts it at the end of a descriptor.
  3. In dma_controller.sv, the descriptor length is just the MM2S_n_BYTES register, a full 32-bit field passed straight through.

So streaming 8×K bytes instead of 64 should accumulate all of K in one invocation and emit the same 8×8 tile. And the packed buffers already suit it — the X run for a fixed m0 and the W run for a fixed n0 are already contiguous and already in k-major order, which is exactly the beat order the array consumes.

The consequence, if it holds on hardware

At 50 µs per call, the 320 px GEMM loop goes from 587 s to about 25 s, and the whole query from 713 s to roughly 165 s. That is 320 px accuracy — top-1 selection 0.532 against today's 0.186 — in less wall-clock time than the 128 px pipeline takes right now. These are projections from the measured 50 µs call cost, not results. §2 exists to turn them into results or kill them.

And why the 64×64 array comes off the table

Your instinct to widen the array was aimed at the right problem — but at the wrong term. Widening cuts arithmetic, and arithmetic is roughly 0.16 µs of a 251 µs invocation. Once the k0 loop is gone, GEMM is 25 s of a 165 s query, so even an infinitely fast array saves at most 15%. A 64×64 array also needs about 442,000 LUTs against roughly 95,900 free — four times the device — and 32×32 exceeds both the free LUTs and the 790 available DSP slices.

This is worth a slide of its own. We considered widening the array, measured what it would buy, and rejected it on the numbers. That reads far better to a hardware audience than building it would have.

§2Phase 0 — prove it, today

One probe, twenty minutes, and the rest of the plan is decided.

team43_kdepth.c is written and compiles clean. It builds a K-major operand pair, computes a software reference, then runs three methods against it at K = 8, 16, 64, 128, 256, 512 and 1024, twenty random operand sets each.

  1. A — the shipping method: K/8 invocations, seed and harvest Y around them.
  2. B — one invocation over the whole K, keeping the current one-read wait.
  3. C — one invocation, but Y_DONE cleared first and then genuinely polled.
The hazard C exists to close

wait_done() polls Y_DONE but nothing ever clears it, so after the first tile it is permanently high and the "poll" is really one syscall's worth of delay — about 50 µs. That is why the pipeline is correct today: a K=8 tile finishes well inside it. A K=1024 tile streams 1024 beats at 50 MHz, about 20.5 µs, plus DMA latency. Still inside, but the margin has become a guess rather than an accident. If B passes and C passes, ship C anyway — it costs one syscall per output tile and does not depend on a coincidence.

Correctness is judged against the software reference, never against method A, so a wrong A cannot make B or C look right. Build and run:

§3Phase 1 — collapse the loop

A flag, not a rewrite. The old path stays reachable all week.

I implement --kdepth N in gemm_run: N K-tiles per invocation, with N=1 reproducing today's behaviour exactly. The default becomes "all of K", and any value in between is available if Gate 0 found a ceiling. Nothing else in the pipeline changes, so a regression is one flag away from being isolated.

  1. Host verification first: --sim is unaffected, and --kdepth 1 must produce byte-identical output to the current binary.
  2. Board at 128 px: the marked image and every ranking line must match your existing result_419974_128.ppm exactly. Identical output, lower time, or we stop.
  3. Then 192 px and 320 px, timed.
Targets — Phase 1 128 px: ≤ 60 s, output identical to today's run.
320 px: ≤ 180 s, and the kitchen image still selects the foreground wine glass at 0.546.
Any mismatch in a ranking line is a failure, not a rounding difference.

Attack in this order, and stop when the demo is comfortable rather than chasing the last second:

  1. VEGA float, 78 s. The requantisation is a float multiply per output element. Replacing it with an integer multiply-and-shift, and SiLU with a lookup table, is the standard fix and should take a large bite out of this.
  2. im2col, 35 s. Currently a scatter per patch. For 1×1 convolutions — which are most of YOLOv8n's parameters — im2col is the identity and can be skipped entirely.
  3. Upload, 20 s. X is re-uploaded per M-chunk; weights already are not. Worth checking whether the chunking can be widened.
Target — Phase 2 320 px query under 120 s. That is a live demo that holds an audience: ask a question, talk through the architecture, show the marked image.

§5Phase 3 — lock the demo and collect evidence

Board access ends 27 August. Everything that needs hardware must be captured by then.

  1. All 14 tasks, on the board, at the chosen canvas. One image per task, terminal log and marked image saved for each. This is the evidence base for every claim in the talk, and we have never had it from hardware.
  2. The paper's Figure 1 contrast, on hardware. Same image, two prompts, two different objects marked. It is the clearest 30 seconds in the whole presentation.
  3. Cold-start rehearsal, timed. Power cycle, network up, files transferred, first query answered. Never measured, and it is the most likely thing to go wrong on stage. The board's SSH host key regenerates every boot, so ./push with StrictHostKeyChecking=no is part of the drill.
  4. Freeze. Tag the working binary and model files. The Stage 3B bitstream and binary stay untouched as the fallback — we are not rebuilding the bitstream at any point in this plan, which is precisely why the risk is bounded.
Targets — Phase 3 14 board logs + 14 marked images.
Cold start measured end to end, with a written checklist that someone else could follow.
A frozen, named build that we do not touch again.

§6Phase 4 — the presentation

Fifteen minutes. It does not exist yet, and it is what you are actually judged on.

The technical work is only worth what the talk conveys. The narrative that the evidence now supports, in order:

  1. The problem. Task-driven selection, the paper, why an edge device is the interesting case. One slide.
  2. The system. SoC, accelerator, what runs where. Emphasise that the board takes an ordinary JPEG and a plain-English prompt and needs nothing else.
  3. The measurement that mattered. Top-1 selection accuracy, the oracle ceiling, and the attribution: detector 72 points, ranker 8. This is the intellectual core and nobody else will have it.
  4. What we found out about our own ranker. 94.7% on clean boxes; 0.394 mAP on the paper's own detections against their published 0.326.
  5. The bottleneck nobody expected. 93% system calls, the RTL read that revealed the k0 loop was never necessary, and the measured result.
  6. What we chose not to build. The array-widening analysis and why the numbers said no.
  7. Live demo.
  8. Honest limits. What is still lost, and what we would do with another month.

The Stage 3B report also needs its timings and accuracy figures replaced — it currently says 528.8 s and 0.063 mAP, both superseded. That is a paper exercise and can happen on the 28th or in Bangalore.
